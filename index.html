    Compare a list

    Surveys rarely ask about one thing. They ask the same question about a whole list — and the interesting answer is usually the ranking.

    An example. This survey asked “Do you favor or oppose expanding each of the following sources of energy in our country?” and then named six: solar, wind, nuclear, offshore drilling, fracking, coal. Asked one at a time those are six separate numbers. Put on one chart they answer a better question: which energy sources do Americans actually want more of, and in what order?

    This screen finds those lists automatically and draws them. Pick a list below and the chart appears straight away.

    Crosstab

    The same idea as the previous screen, but as a table of numbers rather than a picture. Use it when you want the exact figures to quote.

    How a crosstab works. Pick a group on the left and a question on the right. Each row of the table is one group of people, and the numbers along that row show how that group answered, adding to 100%. Reading across a row tells you what one group thinks. Reading down a column compares the groups on one answer.

    Underneath, two numbers judge the table. p is the chance of seeing differences this big if the groups really answered alike — below 0.05 is the usual line for taking a difference seriously. Cramer's V says how big the difference is, from 0 (none) to 1 (total). With a survey of several thousand people almost any table clears the p test, so V is normally the more useful of the two.

    General support vs. local support

    Two questions answered by the same people: whether they support a technology in general, and whether they support it where they live. The difference between those two percentages is the quantity at the center of the NIMBY literature. Because both answers come from the same respondents, the two percentages are correlated, and the comparison uses McNemar's test on the people who answered the two questions differently.

    Using this with the Pew energy survey (Wave 148). That wave asks everyone whether they favor more solar panel farms in our country — the variable called ENV2_d. It does not ask whether they would favor one where they live. What it asks instead, of half the sample, is what they think a local solar farm would do:

    COMMSOLR_a make the landscape unattractive · COMMSOLR_b take up too much space · COMMSOLR_c bring in tax revenue · COMMSOLR_d lower your electricity bill · GRNECON1 help or hurt the local economy

    So a fair comparison is ENV2_d against one of those. Set question A to ENV2_d with Favor as the supporting answer, and question B to COMMSOLR_a with the two No, it would not answers as supporting, since not expecting harm is the pro-solar side of that item.

    Say carefully what you found. This measures people who favor solar nationally but expect a local downside. That is not the same as people who would oppose a local farm — someone can expect a farm to be ugly and still want it built. Wave 148 cannot tell those two people apart, so write it as expected local drawbacks, not as opposition.

    Two practical notes. The local items were asked of only half the sample, so the comparison runs on about 4,300 people rather than 8,638 and the margins of error roughly double. And the solar and wind local items went to different halves, so no one answered both: they cannot be compared within the same people.

    Regression

    A crosstab shows one difference at a time. Rural residents are less supportive of a local project, and rural areas also lean Republican, so a rural difference in a crosstab may be a partisan difference wearing rural clothing. A regression estimates what each thing contributes while the others are held constant, which is what separates the two.

    The two words you need. The dependent variable is the thing you are trying to explain — the answer you think depends on something else. The independent variables are the things you think might explain it: age, party, education, where someone lives.

    The model reports what each independent variable contributes with the others held still, so "rural" no longer includes the part that was really about party.
